    Decisions from forecast draws

    A forecast gives a range of outcomes. Often you then have to commit to one number: a reserve, an order quantity, a staffing level. This page shows how to pick the number with the lowest expected cost, given what being wrong in each direction costs you.

    Read it if you already have predictive draws, from a rustmc forecast or anywhere else, and the cost of a shortfall differs from the cost of a surplus.

    Reserve for next month

    Next month's rebate liability is forecast at about $7.5M, with a 95% interval of about $5M to $10M. Each dollar under-reserved costs five times what a dollar over-reserved costs. How much should you reserve?

    The forecast below comes from a local-level model on the log scale, so amounts stay positive. The history is illustrative.

    import numpy as np
import rustmc as rmc

history = np.array([
    5.07, 7.27, 6.74, 5.82, 6.97, 6.01, 7.62, 5.85, 6.01, 5.44, 5.82, 7.15,
    6.67, 8.81, 7.67, 5.28, 5.31, 8.72, 8.25, 7.91, 10.08, 7.87, 6.36, 6.14,
    8.82, 7.43, 7.84, 7.69, 6.86, 6.21, 7.10, 8.09, 7.07, 7.23, 7.42, 7.69,
])  # millions of dollars
model = rmc.BayesianLocalLevel(
    process_variance_prior=rmc.InverseGammaPrior(shape=3.0, scale=0.004),
    observation_variance_prior=rmc.InverseGammaPrior(shape=3.0, scale=0.04),
    initial_mean=float(np.log(history[0])),
    initial_variance=1.0,
)
fit = model.fit(np.log(history), chains=4, draws=2000, warmup=1000, seed=7)
print(fit.summary())

# Joint paths for the next three months, shaped (chain, draw, month).
forecast = rmc.ForecastDraws(np.exp(fit.forecast(steps=3, seed=8).observation_samples))
lower, upper = forecast.interval(0.95)  # equal-tailed, pointwise

loss = rmc.AsymmetricLinearLoss(under=5, over=1)
reserve = forecast.optimal_decision(loss, steps=[0])
print(reserve.decision)            # about 8.65
print(reserve.quantile)            # 5 / (5 + 1) = 0.833
print(reserve.expected_loss, reserve.expected_loss_mcse)
print(reserve.decision_mcse)       # Monte Carlo standard error of the reserve

    The answer is about $8.65M: the 83rd percentile of the forecast. With these costs, reserving the mean ($7.49M) has an expected cost of about 2.96 against 2.01 at the optimum. With over=1 read as one dollar of cost per dollar over-reserved, these are expected costs in millions of dollars.

    This is the newsvendor result. With a cost of under per unit short and over per unit in excess, the expected cost is lowest at the under / (under + over) quantile of the outcome. Only the ratio of the two costs matters for the decision.

    To see what another reserve would cost:

    for amount in [7.49, 8.65, 10.18]:
    cost = forecast.expected_loss(amount, loss, steps=[0])
    print(amount, cost.value, cost.mcse)

    Losses

    Loss Cost of deciding a when the outcome is y Best decision
    AsymmetricLinearLoss(under=u, over=o) u * (y - a) if y > a, else o * (a - y) the u / (u + o) quantile
    SquaredLoss() (y - a) ** 2 the mean
    AbsoluteLoss() abs(y - a) the median
    PiecewiseLinearLoss(shortfall, loss) linear between knots in y - a best candidate on a grid
    a function f(a, outcomes) whatever it returns, one value per draw best candidate on a grid

    For the first three, decide uses the known optimum: a quantile, the mean or the median of the draws. The quantile follows the same rule as every rustmc interval: linear interpolation between sorted draws, which matches NumPy's default np.quantile. That can sit up to one gap between neighbouring draws away from the exact minimiser of the average loss, which costs at most (u + o) / n times that gap for n draws. The mean and the median are exact.

    Totals over several months

    A reserve for the next quarter covers the total of three months. Pass the months to total with steps, numbered from 0:

    quarter = forecast.optimal_decision(loss, steps=range(3))
monthly = sum(forecast.optimal_decision(loss, steps=[m]).decision for m in range(3))
print(quarter.decision, monthly)   # about 25.0 against 26.1

    Each total is summed within one forecast path, so it keeps the dependence between months. That is why it needs joint paths, and why it differs from adding three monthly reserves: the 83rd percentile of a total is not the total of the 83rd percentiles. Here the sum of monthly reserves costs about 4.51 in expectation against 4.29 for the quarter's own optimum.

    steps=range(3) gives the same totals as step 2 of forecast.cumulative_observation_samples. Any set of months works, in any order, such as steps=[3, 5]. Draws with a series axis must be combined first, for example with ForecastDraws.aggregate().

    Your own loss

    A piecewise-linear loss describes tiered costs. Here a shortfall beyond $0.5M costs 10 per dollar instead of 5. Its knots are in the shortfall outcome - decision, and it continues along its first and last segments past the outer knots:

    tiered = rmc.PiecewiseLinearLoss(shortfall=[-1.0, 0.0, 0.5, 1.5], loss=[1.0, 0.0, 2.5, 12.5])
grid = np.arange(7.0, 12.0, 0.01)
tiered_reserve = forecast.optimal_decision(tiered, steps=[0], candidates=grid)
print(tiered_reserve.decision, tiered_reserve.on_grid_edge)

    Any other loss can be a Python function of the decision and the draws. It receives the decision as a float and the draws as a read-only (chain, draw) array, and must return one loss per draw. Each loss should depend only on the decision and its own draw: the block decisions described below average these losses over parts of each chain.

    def with_penalty(decision, outcomes):
    shortfall = outcomes - decision
    linear = np.where(shortfall > 0, 5 * shortfall, -shortfall)
    return linear + 3.0 * (shortfall > 1.0)  # a fixed cost for missing by over $1M

penalised = forecast.optimal_decision(with_penalty, steps=[0], candidates=grid)
print(penalised.decision)

    rustmc uses no formula for the minimum of these losses, so the answer is the best of the candidates, and it is no finer than their spacing. Without candidates the grid is 201 evenly spaced points from the smallest draw to the largest, or fewer when the draws are constant or span too few distinct values. If the best candidate is the first or last one (or the only one), on_grid_edge is True and the true minimum may lie outside the grid; widen it. A Python loss is called once per candidate, so it runs at Python speed. The built-in losses run in Rust.

    method="grid" searches the candidates for the built-in losses too. Use it when only some decisions are possible, such as reserves in whole $0.25M steps.

    Draws from anywhere

    rmc.decide and rmc.expected_loss take a NumPy array shaped (draw,), (chain, draw) or (chain, draw, horizon), a ForecastDraws, or any rustmc forecast result with observation_samples:

    draws = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(7.5, 1.28, size=(4, 1000))
print(rmc.decide(draws, loss).decision)
print(rmc.expected_loss(draws, 8.7, loss).value)

native = fit.forecast(steps=3, seed=8)  # log scale here; decide on its draws directly
print(rmc.decide(native, rmc.SquaredLoss(), steps=[0]).decision)

    Arrays follow the same exact-conversion rule as model data: booleans, strings, values float64 would round, and non-finite draws raise a ValueError naming the argument. The rule applies to arrays passed to decide and expected_loss directly. A ForecastDraws converts its input with NumPy's float conversion when it is built, so a value float64 would round is rounded there rather than refused.

    How far to trust the answer

    Every number here is an average over a finite set of draws, so each carries Monte Carlo error. The result reports it.

    • expected_loss_mcse (and ExpectedLoss.mcse) is the standard deviation of the per-draw losses divided by the square root of their effective sample size. The effective sample size comes from split chains, as in the mcse_mean column of a fit summary, so autocorrelated MCMC draws are not treated as independent. A single array of draws is treated as one chain in the order given. With fewer than four draws per chain it is nan, unless every loss is equal, when it is zero.
    • decision_mcse is a batch-means standard error of the decision. The best decision is recomputed on contiguous blocks of each chain, at least eight blocks in all when the chains are long enough (halves of each of four chains, quarters of each of two), and batch_decisions holds those block decisions, shaped (chain, block). Blocks from chains that disagree spread further apart, which shows up here. For the pinball loss in 3,000 simulated replicates, this estimate ran 3% to 7% below the observed spread of the decision, so read it as approximate. The run is an ignored test, decision_mcse_calibration_over_3000_replicates in rust_core/src/decision.rs.
    • decision_mcse is nan for a grid search. The best candidate jumps between grid points rather than moving smoothly with the draws, and on a coarse grid the batch-means formula understated the spread of the decision threefold. It is also nan for a quantile at 0 or 1 (the smallest or largest draw). batch_decisions is still reported, and shows how much the choice moves.
    • The expected loss at the chosen decision is biased low. The decision was picked to make that average small on these same draws, and its standard error treats the decision as fixed.

    None of this covers error in the forecast itself. The decision is exactly as good as the predictive distribution behind it, so check that distribution on held-out periods first; see Forecasting workflows. A decision layer also cannot tell you what the costs are: under and over are business inputs, and the answer moves with their ratio.
