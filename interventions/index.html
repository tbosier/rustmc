    Interventions and changepoints

    This page covers TimeDesign, which builds regression columns for events you know about (a contract change, a price change, an outage, a launch) and for changes in trend at dates you do not know. Pass its columns as exog to the forecasting models on the regression page, to StructuralModel, or to the dynamic GLMs. Read it if your series has a break and you want the forecast to account for it.

    A TimeDesign fixes the training window when you build it. Its future(steps) rows come from the same column definitions evaluated at the positions after that window, so they continue the training rows exactly. You never give the forecast rows their own start position, so they cannot be shifted by one or more steps against the training rows, a mistake that is easy to make when building such columns by hand.

    Build a design and fit it

    import numpy as np
import rustmc as rmc

# 100 training steps and 12 to forecast. At step 60 the level jumps by 3 and the
# slope rises by 0.08 per step; step 30 has a one-off drop of 2.
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, steps = 100, 12
t = np.arange(n + steps)
y = (10 + 0.02 * t + 3.0 * (t >= 60) + 0.08 * np.maximum(0, t - 60)
     - 2.0 * (t == 30) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.03, n + steps))
     + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n + steps))

D = rmc.DesignTerm
design = rmc.TimeDesign([
    D.ramp("trend", at=0),            # the ordinary linear trend
    D.step("shift", at=60),           # level shift
    D.ramp("slope_change", at=60),    # change in slope
    D.pulse("outage", at=30),         # one-step effect
], length=n)
print(design.names)   # ['trend', 'shift', 'slope_change', 'outage']

prior = rmc.GaussianCoefficientPrior(np.zeros(4), np.diag([0.01, 25.0, 0.01, 25.0]))
model = rmc.BayesianLocalLevel(rmc.InverseGammaPrior(3, 0.01), rmc.InverseGammaPrior(3, 0.2),
                               initial_mean=10.0, initial_variance=4.0)
fit = model.fit(y[:n], exog=design.training(), coefficient_prior=prior,
                chains=4, draws=500, warmup=500, seed=1)
forecast = fit.forecast(steps, exog=design.future(steps), seed=2)

beta = fit.get_samples_2d()["coefficients"].reshape(-1, design.width)
lower, upper = np.quantile(beta, [0.025, 0.975], axis=0)
for name, low, high in zip(design.names, lower, upper):
    print(f"{name:13s} [{low:6.3f}, {high:6.3f}]")

    Each coefficient gets its own prior variance. The coefficient is in units of y per unit of its column, so the variance is in the square of those units. Choose them for your series. The coefficient intervals are equal-tailed posterior intervals; the forecast intervals are posterior-predictive intervals conditional on the design.

    The terms

    Positions are integers on the same calendar as fourier_design: training row i is position start + i, and start defaults to 0. Every term is a function of the position t alone.

    Term Column at position t The coefficient is
    DesignTerm.step(name, at) 1 if t >= at, else 0 the level shift from at on
    DesignTerm.pulse(name, at) 1 if t == at, else 0 a one-period effect
    DesignTerm.ramp(name, at) max(0, t - at) the change in slope per step after at
    DesignTerm.decay(name, at, rate) rate**(t - at) if t >= at, else 0 the effect at at; it shrinks by rate each step
    DesignTerm.fourier(name, period, harmonics) the fourier_design columns seasonal weights
    DesignTerm.changepoints(name, ...) max(0, t - s) for each changepoint s the change in slope at s
    DesignTerm.regressor(name) values you supply an ordinary regression effect

    A decay term's total effect over all later steps is coefficient / (1 - rate). rate must lie strictly between 0 and 1 and is fixed, not estimated; a pulse is the rate = 0 case and a step the rate = 1 case. A ramp column is 0 at at and 1 one step later, so the trend has no jump; pair it with a step for a jump as well. A ramp at the first training position is a plain linear trend.

    Column names are the term name, except for Fourier terms (annual_sin1, annual_cos1, and so on, with only the cosine at the Nyquist harmonic, as in fourier_design) and changepoint terms (trend@24, one per position). Term names and column names must be unique within a design.

    Where the event falls

    The columns follow their definitions wherever at lies. That has consequences worth knowing before you fit:

    • Before the training window. A step is 1 on every training row, so it is indistinguishable from the level or an intercept; only its prior separates them. A pulse is 0 on every training row, so the data say nothing about it. A ramp keeps counting from at and a decay has already decayed by the first row; both still vary over the training rows, so the data do inform them.
    • At the first training position. A step is again constant over the training rows. A ramp at start is the linear trend.
    • After the training window, inside the forecast horizon. Every training value is 0, so the data say nothing about the effect directly. With independent coefficient priors (a diagonal covariance) its posterior is its prior; with a correlated prior it moves with the coefficients it is correlated with. The forecast carries that uncertainty from at on. Use this for a planned change whose size you can state as a prior. Its future rows switch on at the right step.
    • Late in the training window. Only the few rows after at inform the effect, so its interval is wide.

    From dates to positions

    TimeDesign takes integer positions, not dates. Convert a date with the index of your series:

    months = np.arange("2019-01", "2026-01", dtype="datetime64[M]")
contract = int(np.searchsorted(months, np.datetime64("2023-04")))
print(contract)   # 51: the 52nd month of the series

    Check that the date is actually in the index; searchsorted returns the insertion point for a missing date without complaint. With pandas, index.get_loc(date) raises instead.

    Future rows and your own regressors

    design.training() returns (length, width) rows for positions start .. start + length - 1, and design.future(steps) returns (steps, width) rows for the positions right after them. Build the design once and use the same object for both. A column you measure yourself, such as price or promotions, is a DesignTerm.regressor, and its values are passed by name:

    promo = rng.binomial(1, 0.3, n + steps).astype(float)
design = rmc.TimeDesign([
    D.step("shift", at=60),
    D.fourier("annual", period=12, harmonics=2),
    D.regressor("promo"),
], length=n)
X = design.training(regressors={"promo": promo[:n]})
X_future = design.future(steps, regressors={"promo": promo[n:]})
print(X.shape, X_future.shape)   # (100, 6) (12, 6)

    Regressor values need exactly one finite number per row requested, and follow the same exact-conversion rule as every other array argument. A missing name, an unknown name, a wrong length, NaN, a boolean array or an integer float64 cannot hold raises a ValueError naming the regressor. An invalid term or design raises a plain ValueError naming the term or the problem, as fourier_design does. As there, an argument of the wrong Python type, or an integer out of range (a negative count or length, a position beyond 64 bits), raises Python's own TypeError or OverflowError instead.

    What the design cannot check is the data you pair it with. The generated columns are right for positions start .., but the regressor values you pass for the future must be the values for those positions, and the observations you fit must be the length observations the design describes. The models do refuse an exog whose row count differs from the observations or from the forecast horizon.

    For a rolling-origin evaluation with backtest, build the design over every observation and pass design.training() as exog; backtest slices it at each origin, and the deterministic columns need no future information. A changepoint grid is the exception: its default positions come from the whole window, so fix them with at= if each fold must not see the full length of the series.

    Changepoints in the trend

    For a trend that may change slope at dates you do not know, DesignTerm.changepoints adds one ramp column per candidate position, as Prophet does. With a base linear trend D.ramp("trend", at=0) the fitted trend is piecewise linear: its slope after step t is the trend coefficient plus every changepoint coefficient at or before t.

    # The slope is 0.05, then 0.20 from step 35, then -0.05 from step 65.
slope = np.where(t < 35, 0.05, np.where(t < 65, 0.20, -0.05))
y2 = 5 + np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(slope[:-1])]) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n + steps)

trend_design = rmc.TimeDesign([D.ramp("trend", at=0), D.changepoints("cp", count=10)], length=n)
print(trend_design.changepoints)   # {'cp': [8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 47, 55, 63, 71, 79]}
k = trend_design.width
trend_fit = rmc.BayesianLocalLevel(rmc.InverseGammaPrior(3, 0.002), rmc.InverseGammaPrior(3, 0.2),
                                   initial_mean=5.0, initial_variance=4.0).fit(
    y2[:n], exog=trend_design.training(),
    coefficient_prior=rmc.GaussianCoefficientPrior(np.zeros(k), 0.01 * np.eye(k)),
    chains=4, draws=300, warmup=300, seed=3)
trend_forecast = trend_fit.forecast(steps, exog=trend_design.future(steps), seed=4)
deltas = trend_fit.get_samples_2d()["coefficients"].reshape(-1, k)[:, 1:].mean(axis=0)
print(dict(zip(trend_design.names[1:], deltas.round(3).tolist())))

    Give either explicit positions with at=[...] or a grid with count=. The grid places count evenly spaced positions after first, ending at first + floor(fraction * (last - first)), and rounds each to the nearest integer (halves up). first and last default to the first and last training positions, and fraction defaults to 0.8, Prophet's default: candidates stop before the last fifth of the history, where a slope change has few points to estimate it but moves the forecast most. first itself is not a candidate, because a hinge there is the base trend. design.changepoints shows the resolved positions. A grid needs at least count positions in its window.

    This is shrinkage, not detection. The coefficient priors in these models are Gaussian, so the changepoint coefficients are shrunk towards zero the way ridge regression shrinks, not the way a sparse prior does. In the test suite's four seeded versions of the example above, the slope change of +0.15 at step 35, which lies between candidates 32 and 40, was split between them (posterior means 0.03–0.14 at 32 and 0.06–0.13 at 40), and candidates where nothing changed still had posterior means of up to about ±0.07. Averaged over the middle of each segment (steps 5–29, 40–59 and 70–94), the fitted slope was within 0.03 of the truth. Near a change it can be far off: at step 70, five steps after the slope fell to −0.05, the fitted slope was still between +0.02 and +0.04 on all four seeds, because the next candidate is at 71. The individual coefficients do not say where a break happened. No part of this estimates a break date or tests whether a break occurred.

    A sparse prior such as the Laplace or horseshoe, which would push most changepoint coefficients to near zero and leave a few large, is not available. The regression models sample coefficients jointly with the states from a Gaussian prior, and adding a scale-mixture prior would change that sampler; it is not part of this feature.

    The prior variance sets how far the trend can bend: a larger variance lets the fit follow noise, a smaller one flattens real changes. Changepoint coefficients are in units of y per step, so a variance that suits monthly data is far too large for daily data. Assess the choice with a rolling-origin backtest.

    Using a design with other models

    StructuralModel reads regression columns in the order of its regression components. design.term_slice(name) gives the columns of one term, so you can give each term its own component, prior, and, if you want, time-varying coefficients:

    C, V = rmc.StructuralComponent, rmc.VarianceParameter
components = [C.level("level", V.inverse_gamma(3, 0.01), initial_mean=10.0, initial_variance=4.0)]
for term in design.terms:
    width = len(design.names[design.term_slice(term.name)])
    components.append(C.regression(term.name, initial_mean=np.zeros(width),
                                   initial_covariance=9.0 * np.eye(width)))
structural = rmc.StructuralModel(components, observation_variance=V.inverse_gamma(3, 0.2))
structural_fit = structural.fit(y[:n], exog=X, chains=2, draws=300, warmup=300, seed=5)
structural_forecast = structural_fit.forecast(steps, exog=X_future, seed=6)

    Keep the terms in the design in the same order as the components.

    The dynamic GLMs take exog[group][time][feature]. Broadcast the same design to every group:

    groups = 3
counts = rng.poisson(np.exp(1.0 + 0.7 * (t[:n] >= 60)), size=(groups, n)).astype(float)
count_design = rmc.TimeDesign([D.step("shift", at=60)], length=n)
glm_fit = rmc.BayesianDynamicPoisson(initial_mean=1.0, process_sd=0.05).fit(
    counts, exog=np.broadcast_to(count_design.training(), (groups, n, 1)).copy(),
    chains=2, draws=300, warmup=300, seed=7)
glm_forecast = glm_fit.forecast(
    steps, exog=np.broadcast_to(count_design.future(steps), (groups, steps, 1)).copy(), seed=8)

    The dynamic GLMs add their own intercept, so do not include a step that is 1 on every training row. Their coefficient prior is one coefficient_sd for every column, on the linear-predictor scale (the log rate for Poisson); ramp and changepoint columns grow with time, so scale them, or prefer steps, pulses and decays there.

    What the tests show

    tests/test_time_design.py checks the exact matrix of each term, including events before the window, at position 0, on the last training row and inside the forecast horizon, and that future() rows equal the matching rows of one longer design for every term type. On a simulated series with a level shift of 3 and a slope change of 0.08 at step 60 (the first example above, with four seeds):

    • the 95% intervals for both effects contain the true values and exclude zero, and the coefficient R-hat is below 1.01;
    • fitted to the same series without the break, both intervals contain zero;
    • over eight seeds, the post-break forecast CRPS is 0.20 with the columns and 0.35 without them, and lower on every seed;
    • on one series, a rolling-origin backtest from four origins after the break gives CRPS 0.16 with the columns, 0.32 without, and 0.71 for the naive baseline.

    For the piecewise-linear trend above, the changepoint basis gives forecast CRPS 0.21 against 0.75 without it, over four seeds. One seeded StructuralModel fit and one BayesianDynamicPoisson fit each recover a level shift from the same kind of design, with 95% intervals that contain the truth and exclude zero.

    These are checks on simulated data built to match the model. They do not show that a real series has the breaks you encode, or that the dates are right.

    Limitations

    • You supply the dates. Nothing here detects a break or estimates its date.
    • Changepoint shrinkage is Gaussian (ridge-like), with no sparse prior; see above.
    • A decay term's rate is fixed, not estimated.
    • Positions are integers; convert dates yourself.
    • In the changepoint test, the local level's variance mixed slowly (R-hat 1.04–1.19 at 300 draws) while the coefficients mixed well (R-hat below 1.01). This resembles the slow variance mixing the project roadmap describes for the local linear trend; check fit.diagnostics().
    • A column that is constant or zero on every training row, such as a step on or before the first training row or any event after the last, is identified only by its prior (see where the event falls). Check the design before fitting if the date could be wrong: a column of zeros is not an error.
